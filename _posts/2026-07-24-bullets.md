## Bullets, bullets, bullets everywhere

Not too long ago, a recruiter published an [article on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/activity-7480161603125170176-SJkL?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAAFQz0IBrfqAj0u0OZbY5La1aYnXI22VYs0) asking candidates not to submit resumes containing "eighty-plus technologies crammed into the skills section." I'm not going to repeat everything he said, and whether I agree with him or not is irrelevant. I'll just say that, in his opinion, it's better to have 10 skills you can defend than to list 80 skills you can spell.

Early in my career as a software engineer, I learned Fortran in school. It was my very first programming language. I had no idea how computers worked, even less how to program them. Later on, I learned 8080 assembly language programming in school because it was a completely different paradigm. In both cases, I needed a very solid foundation so I could understand how computers work and how I could make better use of them. From then on, everything I learned in computer science, from firmware to operating systems and from device drivers to applications, compilers with linkers and loaders, dozens upon dozens of languages, APIs, object models, and frameworks, and so many technologies and architectures available, all of which I can explain in detail, I learned by myself. I had mentors and coaches, books, cheatsheets, online references, and tutorials, but especially got burned while learning. {% include pullquote.html quote="Learning is not memorizing the syntax or a diagram, but building something practical to solidify the concepts you have acquired." %}

Project management is what I do now. I couldn't learn it in books, courses, or home lab. Project management is an art and a science; the science is the science, but the art…

When you apply for a given position at a regular company, you'll encounter your worst enemy: the ATS. Applicant tracking systems are an important component of the candidate selection and hiring process, but some of them have a stupid module that scans the resume (or application form), matches keywords with the job requirements, and rates the candidate based on the percentage of matches. Depending on the threshold defined by whoever calls the shots, a number of candidates are automatically excluded. {% include pullquote.html quote="That's how an application you submitted on a Saturday morning is rejected on the same day before lunch." %}

Why does that stupid module even exist? Because there are hundreds, if not thousands, of people applying for the very same position. Furthermore, there are companies that want to hire a new individual with a given profile. They can't find a suitable candidate internally, so they call their contract houses to bring in candidates, which in turn will call their own subcontractors. If the company can't find the right fit, they'll call their class B tiers. And then class C. That's how I have a multitude of recruiters (sometimes from the same company) offering me the very same position (but the lower the tier, the lower the pay).

Not too long ago, I was hiring 3 scrum masters. We received hundreds of applications. I summarily ignored the rating given by that pesky module. Instead, {% include pullquote.html quote="I wanted to give all candidates the dignity of having their resumes evaluated by a real human being." %} It took me considerable time to read every single one. At the end, it paid off! Not only I was able to hire 3 extremely talented professionals, but I also got to meet a lot of very exciting people.

How can you, as a candidate, circumvent this unfair practice? Easy: throw in bullets and more bullets. If a given company is looking for a new change management manager, but you have `change and release management` listed on your skill section, then you'll need to split it in two, otherwise the ATS will send you packing.

## Requirements are ludicrous

These are just a few examples. I'm not going to dignify these companies by mentioning their names.

**July 2026**. A technology giant is looking for a [Senior Software Developer with 10+ years of experience with Claude Code](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/itmentor_activity-7477810433593348096-c7qy?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAAFQz0IBrfqAj0u0OZbY5La1aYnXI22VYs0). Considering that Claude Code was released in [February 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_(AI)#Claude_Code), then either they want to hire one of the original engineers, or this requisition is at least 9 years ahead of its time.

**July 2026**. A consulting giant is looking for a [Data & AI Partner Solution Lead](https://lnkd.in/eXXMTeun) with 8+ years of direct experience with OpenAI APIs, Azure OpenAI Service, or similar large language model platforms required. They also want a [Transformation Partner Solution Lead](https://lnkd.in/e8N6jujZ) with 8+ years with organization implications of AI. [OpenAI was founded in 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI), but [ChatGPT was released in 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChatGPT). Assuming that's what they meant, then the only individuals who qualify would be the company founders (Sam Altman et al), who I doubt are looking for another job, even less settling for only a mere 6-digit salary.

It doesn't bother me seeing fly-by-night organizations posting ridiculous job requisitions, but when reputable corporations such as the above do it, it means they have lost their minds. What does this tell you about the work that's expecting you after you're accepted? Or the company culture? Or lack thereof?
